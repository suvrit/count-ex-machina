\cxappendixsection{Additional results for the Lorentzian Jensen--Bregman principle}
\label{sec:lorentzianaudit}

Theorem~\ref{thm:volume-distance} and Corollary~\ref{thm:lorentzian} refute two statements by exhibiting a
single witness.  Both statements, however, had been posed together with a
proposed proof, and a witness alone does not say which step of that proof is
wrong.  This appendix records the audit: the formulation as posed, the parts of
the argument that are correct, the identity that is not, an elementary
two-dimensional contradiction to it, and the enclosure procedure that certifies
the witness in exact arithmetic.  A final subsection gives the positive
counterpart: on hyperbolic polynomials the refuted principle is a theorem, and
the witness is not hyperbolic.

\subsection*{The formulation, and the verdict}

Let $\mathcal C\subset\R^m$ be an open convex cone, let $G:\mathcal C\to(0,\infty)$, and put
\[
F=-\log G,\qquad
\J_G(x,y):=\frac{F(x)+F(y)}2-F\!\left(\frac{x+y}2\right)
=\log G\!\left(\frac{x+y}2\right)-\frac{\log G(x)+\log G(y)}2 .
\]
The proposed master theorem asserted that if $G$ is homogeneous and Lorentzian
then $d_G:=\sqrt{\J_G}$ is a semimetric; the geometric specialization asserted
that $d_{\Vol}(K,L):=\bigl[\log\Vol(\frac{K+L}2)-\frac12\log\Vol(K)-\frac12\log\Vol(L)\bigr]^{1/2}$
is a semimetric on convex bodies.  The audit finds:

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\roman*)},leftmargin=2.1em]
\item the midpoint integral identity and the homogeneous primitive decomposition
      used in the proposed proof are correct;
\item the claimed Gram-determinant identity for the derivative of a Hessian
      Rayleigh quotient is false --- its two sides have incompatible homogeneity
      in the line direction;
\item the implication ``$G$ Lorentzian $\Rightarrow\sqrt{\J_G}$ a semimetric''
      is false already for a strictly Lorentzian bivariate cubic
      (Corollary~\ref{thm:lorentzian});
\item the convex-body specialization is false as well, since by Shephard's
      theorem that cubic is the volume polynomial of two bodies in $\R^3$
      (Theorem~\ref{thm:volume-distance});
\item consequently the proposed proof cannot be repaired by supplying the
      omitted Gram-determinant calculation: the omission conceals a false
      identity, not a technical gap.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{What the proposed proof gets right}

\begin{lemma}[Midpoint Green-kernel identity]\label{lem:lor-midpoint}
Let $F\in C^2(\mathcal C)$ be convex and $x,y\in\mathcal C$.  With $m=(x+y)/2$ and $h=y-x$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lor-midpoint}
\frac{F(x)+F(y)}2-F(m)
=\frac18\int_{-1}^{1}(1-|t|)\,\mathrm D^2F\!\left(m+\tfrac t2h\right)[h,h]\,dt .
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Put $\varphi(s)=F(m+sh)$.  Taylor's formula with integral remainder gives, for $a\ge0$,
$\varphi(\pm a)=\varphi(0)\pm a\varphi'(0)+\int_0^a(a-u)\varphi''(\pm u)\,du$.
Taking $a=1/2$, adding and halving,
\[
\frac{\varphi(1/2)+\varphi(-1/2)}2-\varphi(0)
=\frac12\int_0^{1/2}\Bigl(\frac12-u\Bigr)\bigl(\varphi''(u)+\varphi''(-u)\bigr)du,
\]
and $\varphi''(u)=\mathrm D^2F(m+uh)[h,h]$ with $t=\pm2u$ gives \eqref{eq:lor-midpoint}.
\end{proof}

For fixed $u$ write $q_u(r):=\mathrm D^2F(r)[u,u]$ for the Hessian Rayleigh quotient;
the monotonicity argument in the proposed proof is an argument about
$\delta\mapsto\mathrm D_\delta q_u(r)$.

\begin{proposition}[Exact third-order derivative]\label{prop:lor-derivative}
Let $G\in C^3(\mathcal C)$ be positive.  For all $r\in\mathcal C$ and $u,\delta\in\R^m$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lor-derivative}
\mathrm D_\delta q_u(r)=-\mathrm D^3(\log G)(r)[u,u,\delta]
=\frac{2G_uG_{u\delta}}{G^2}-\frac{2G_u^2G_\delta}{G^3}
 -\frac{G_{uu\delta}}{G}+\frac{G_{uu}G_\delta}{G^2},
\end{equation}
all derivatives being evaluated at $r$.  In particular $\delta\mapsto\mathrm D_\delta q_u(r)$
is linear, hence odd and homogeneous of degree one in $\delta$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Directional derivatives commute, so
$\mathrm D_\delta q_u=-\mathrm D_\delta\mathrm D_u^2\log G=-\mathrm D_u^2\mathrm D_\delta\log G$,
which is the first equality.  For the second, $q_u=G_u^2/G^2-G_{uu}/G$; differentiating
the two terms along $\delta$ gives $2G_uG_{u\delta}/G^2-2G_u^2G_\delta/G^3$ and
$-G_{uu\delta}/G+G_{uu}G_\delta/G^2$ respectively.  Linearity is visible in
$\mathrm D^3(\log G)(r)[u,u,\cdot]$.
\end{proof}

Now let $G$ be homogeneous of degree $d\ge2$ and set
\[
\mathcal Q_r(v,w):=\frac{\mathrm D^2G(r)[v,w]}{d(d-1)},\qquad
\mathcal T_r(v,w,z):=\frac{\mathrm D^3G(r)[v,w,z]}{d(d-1)},
\]
so that Euler's identities read $\mathcal Q_r(r,r)=G(r)$, $\mathcal Q_r(v,r)=G_v(r)/d$ and
$\mathcal T_r(v,w,r)=(d-2)\mathcal Q_r(v,w)$.  With
$\alpha=\mathcal Q_r(u,r)/G(r)$ and $U=u-\alpha r$ --- so that $\mathcal Q_r(U,r)=0$ ---
the decomposition into primitive coordinates is the following.

\begin{lemma}[Primitive decomposition]\label{lem:lor-primitive}
$\displaystyle q_u(r)=\frac1d\left(\frac{G_u(r)}{G(r)}\right)^2-\frac{d(d-1)}{G(r)}\mathcal Q_r(U,U).$
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
By Euler, $\alpha=G_u/(dG)$, so
$\mathcal Q_r(U,U)=\mathcal Q_r(u,u)-2\alpha\mathcal Q_r(u,r)+\alpha^2\mathcal Q_r(r,r)
=\mathcal Q_r(u,u)-G_u^2/(d^2G)$.  Hence
$\mathrm D^2G[u,u]=d(d-1)\mathcal Q_r(U,U)+\frac{d-1}d\frac{G_u^2}G$, and substituting
into $q_u=G_u^2/G^2-\mathrm D^2G[u,u]/G$ gives the claim.
\end{proof}

Writing $\beta=\mathcal Q_r(\delta,r)/G(r)$ and $V=\delta-\beta r$, expanding
\eqref{eq:lor-derivative} in these coordinates produces an exact four-term
expression in $\mathcal Q_r$ and $\mathcal T_r$, whose leading term is
$-d(d-1)\mathcal T_r(U,U,V)/G(r)$.  Only one feature of it is needed below: like
\eqref{eq:lor-derivative} itself, it is odd and degree-one in $\delta$.

\subsection*{Where the proposed proof fails}

The step that was omitted asserted a formula of the schematic shape
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lor-gram}
\mathrm D_\delta q_u(r)=C(r)\det
\begin{pmatrix}
\langle U,U\rangle_r&\langle U,V\rangle_r\\
\langle V,U\rangle_r&\langle V,V\rangle_r
\end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
with $V$ the primitive projection of $\delta$, the inner product derived from
$-\mathcal Q_r$, and $C(r)$ independent of $\delta$.  No such formula exists.

\begin{proposition}[Homogeneity obstruction]\label{prop:lor-obstruction}
No identity of the form \eqref{eq:lor-gram} with $C(r)$ independent of $\delta$
can hold for all line directions $\delta$, except where both sides vanish
identically.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
By Proposition~\ref{prop:lor-derivative} the left side is odd and homogeneous of degree one
in $\delta$.  The projection $V$ is linear in $\delta$, so the Gram determinant
on the right is even and homogeneous of degree two.  Replacing $\delta$ by
$c\delta$ multiplies the two sides by $c$ and $c^2$; replacing $\delta$ by
$-\delta$ changes the sign of the left side only.  Both are possible together
only if each side vanishes.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[The same parity kills the shortcut]\label{rem:lor-parity}
The proposed one-sided bound $\mathcal T_r(U,U,V)\le0$ for every line direction
fails for the same reason: replacing $\delta$ by $-\delta$ replaces $V$ by $-V$
and flips the sign, so a bound valid for both orientations forces
$\mathcal T_r(U,U,V)\equiv0$.  Likewise $\mathrm D_\delta q_u(r)\ge0$ for every
$\delta$ would force every directional derivative to vanish.  A monotonicity
statement here must select an oriented cone of directions; it cannot come from
an even Gram determinant.
\end{remark}

The failure is not confined to exotic $G$.  Take axis-aligned rectangles
$X_a=X_c=[0,1]^2$ and $X_b=[0,2]\times[0,3]$, fix $\theta=1/2$, and for
$0<s<1/2$ put $r(s)=(1-\theta)e_a+se_b+(\theta-s)e_c$.  Since $X_a=X_c$,
\[
M(s)=\nlsum_ir_i(s)X_i=(1-s)X_a+sX_b=[0,1+s]\times[0,1+2s],
\qquad G(r(s))=\Vol(M(s))=(1+s)(1+2s).
\]
For $u=e_b-e_a$ the two side lengths have directional increments $1$ and $2$, so
with $F=-\log G$,
\[
q_u(r(s))=\frac1{(1+s)^2}+\frac4{(1+2s)^2},
\qquad
\frac{d}{ds}q_u(r(s))=-\frac2{(1+s)^3}-\frac{16}{(1+2s)^3}<0 .
\]
The proposed mixed-volume version of \eqref{eq:lor-gram} carried a prefactor
$n(n-1)(n-2)$, which vanishes at $n=2$ and so predicts a zero derivative.  The
stated mixed-volume calculation is therefore already false for two rectangles in
the plane.

\subsection*{The exact enclosure}

The witness of Lemma~\ref{lem:lorentzian-cubic} is three logarithms and three square
roots, none of them rational.  The separation is nevertheless certified without
a floating-point step, through the following enclosure.

\begin{lemma}[Rational enclosure for the logarithm]\label{lem:lor-log}
Let $x>1$ be rational, put $z=(x-1)/(x+1)\in(0,1)$ and let $N\ge1$.  Then
\[
L_N(x):=2\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\frac{z^{2k+1}}{2k+1}
\;<\;\log x\;<\;
L_N(x)+\frac{2z^{2N+1}}{(2N+1)(1-z^2)},
\]
and every quantity displayed is rational.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Since $x=(1+z)/(1-z)$ we have $\log x=2\operatorname{artanh}z=2\sum_{k\ge0}z^{2k+1}/(2k+1)$,
all terms positive, which gives the lower bound.  For $k\ge N$ we have
$(2k+1)^{-1}\le(2N+1)^{-1}$, so the tail is at most
$\frac2{2N+1}\sum_{k\ge N}z^{2k+1}=\frac{2z^{2N+1}}{(2N+1)(1-z^2)}$.
\end{proof}

For the square roots, a positive rational $q$ and a decimal scale $S=10^P$ give
the exact two-sided bound $m/S\le\sqrt q<(m+1)/S$ with
$m=\lfloor\sqrt{qS^2}\rfloor$ computed by integer square root.  Combining the
two, with a lower enclosure for $\sqrt{\J_G(p,r)}$ and upper enclosures for
$\sqrt{\J_G(p,q)}$ and $\sqrt{\J_G(q,r)}$, at $N=100$ series terms and $P=45$
digits, yields the bound quoted in \eqref{eq:lorentzian-gap},
\[
d_G(p,r)-d_G(p,q)-d_G(q,r)>\frac{7156704176613847159228106087505875849130467}{10^{45}} .
\]
This is what \path{counterexamples/lorentzian-jensen/verify.py} recomputes: it
forms the three ratios $R_{pq},R_{qr},R_{pr}$ as exact fractions, bounds each
logarithm by Lemma~\ref{lem:lor-log}, encloses the three square roots on the
$10^{-45}$ grid by integer square root, and asserts that the resulting
difference is positive.  Only \texttt{Fraction} and \texttt{isqrt} are used, so
the final assertion is a statement about integers.  The strict Lorentzianity of
$G$ and the Shephard inequalities are not part of that computation; both are
finite rational comparisons, carried out in the proofs of
Lemma~\ref{lem:lorentzian-cubic} and Theorem~\ref{thm:volume-distance}.

\subsection*{What survives}

The counterexample leaves the following untouched.  In one dimension,
\citet{ChenChenRao} characterize metricity of $\sqrt{\J_F}$ by convexity of
$\log F''$, and that result extends to separable sums and, more generally, to
nonnegative superpositions of one-dimensional generators.  The Jensen--Bregman
log-determinant divergence is the S-divergence, whose square root is a metric
\citep{SraSdiv} --- a fact that does not follow from Lorentzianity and is not
touched by the witness.  What the witness does remove is the hope that
Lorentzianity of $G=e^{-F}$ is by itself the higher-dimensional criterion.

\begin{proposition}[Nonnegative superposition]\label{prop:lor-superposition}
Let $(\Omega,\mu)$ be a measure space; for each $\omega$ let $I_\omega\subset\R$
be an interval, $f_\omega:I_\omega\to\R$ convex with
$\sqrt{\J_{f_\omega}}$ a semimetric on $I_\omega$, and
$\ell_\omega:\mathcal C\to I_\omega$ affine.  If
$F(x):=\int_\Omega f_\omega(\ell_\omega(x))\,d\mu(\omega)$ is finite, then
$\sqrt{\J_F}$ is a semimetric on $\mathcal C$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Affineness of $\ell_\omega$ and linearity of the Jensen gap in $F$ give
$\J_F(x,y)=\int_\Omega d_\omega(\ell_\omega(x),\ell_\omega(y))^2d\mu$ with
$d_\omega=\sqrt{\J_{f_\omega}}$.  For $x,y,z$, the pointwise triangle inequality
followed by Minkowski's inequality in $L^2(\mu)$ gives
$\sqrt{\J_F(x,z)}\le\sqrt{\J_F(x,y)}+\sqrt{\J_F(y,z)}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Linewise necessary condition]\label{prop:lor-linewise}
Let $F\in C^4(\mathcal C)$ be strictly convex with $\sqrt{\J_F}$ a metric on $\mathcal C$.
Then for every $x\in\mathcal C$ and every $h\ne0$ with $x+th\in\mathcal C$ on an interval $I$,
the map $t\mapsto\log\bigl(\mathrm D^2F(x+th)[h,h]\bigr)$ is convex on $I$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The restriction $f(t)=F(x+th)$ inherits the metric, so the one-dimensional
necessity theorem of \citet{ChenChenRao} gives convexity of $\log f''$, and
$f''(t)=\mathrm D^2F(x+th)[h,h]$.
\end{proof}

Linewise log-convexity is thus necessary, and Proposition~\ref{prop:lor-superposition} is a
sufficient condition of a different kind; what is missing --- and what the
witness shows Lorentzianity does not supply --- is a genuinely nonseparable
condition that controls arbitrary triangles.  The next subsection supplies one.

\subsection*{The hyperbolic transfer principle}

Replacing ``Lorentzian'' by ``hyperbolic'' turns the refuted principle into a
theorem.  Let $p:\R^n\to\R$ be homogeneous of degree $m\ge1$ and hyperbolic with
respect to $e$, normalized so that $p(e)>0$, and let $\Lambda_{++}$ be the open
hyperbolicity cone containing $e$.  By \citet{Garding1959},
$\Lambda_{++}$ is an open convex cone, $p$ is hyperbolic with respect to each of
its points, and $F:=p^{1/m}$ is positive, homogeneous of degree one and concave
on it.  The other external input is the Helton--Vinnikov theorem
\citep{HeltonVinnikov2007,LewisParriloRamana2005}, in the normalized form: a
ternary form $q$ of degree $m$, hyperbolic with respect to $\mathbf 1=(1,1,1)$
with $q(\mathbf 1)>0$, admits real symmetric $A_1,A_2,A_3$ with
$A_1+A_2+A_3=I_m$ and $q(r)=q(\mathbf 1)\det(r_1A_1+r_2A_2+r_3A_3)$.  Three
points at a time is exactly what a triangle inequality needs.

\begin{lemma}[Ternary restriction]\label{lem:lor-ternary}
Fix $x_1,x_2,x_3\in\Lambda_{++}$, put $T(r):=r_1x_1+r_2x_2+r_3x_3$ and
$q:=p\circ T$.  Then $q$ is homogeneous of degree $m$ and hyperbolic with
respect to $\mathbf 1$, and each coordinate vector $\varepsilon_i\in\R^3$ lies in
the hyperbolicity cone of $q$ containing $\mathbf 1$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Homogeneity is immediate, and $w:=T(\mathbf 1)=x_1+x_2+x_3\in\Lambda_{++}$
because the cone is convex.  So $p$ is hyperbolic with respect to $w$, and for
every $r\in\R^3$ the univariate $\lambda\mapsto q(r+\lambda\mathbf 1)
=p(T(r)+\lambda w)$ has only real zeros; that is hyperbolicity with respect to
$\mathbf 1$.  For the second claim, the path
$\gamma_i(t)=(1-t)\varepsilon_i+t\mathbf 1$ satisfies
$T(\gamma_i(t))=x_i+t\sum_{j\ne i}x_j\in\Lambda_{++}$, so $q(\gamma_i(t))>0$ on
$[0,1]$: it joins $\varepsilon_i$ to $\mathbf 1$ inside the positive component.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Positive definite representatives]\label{lem:lor-pdrep}
In that setting the matrices $A_1,A_2,A_3$ supplied by Helton--Vinnikov are
positive definite, and for all $i,j$,
\[
p(x_i)=q(\mathbf 1)\det A_i,\qquad
p\Bigl(\frac{x_i+x_j}2\Bigr)=q(\mathbf 1)\det\Bigl(\frac{A_i+A_j}2\Bigr).
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Along the path of Lemma~\ref{lem:lor-ternary},
$L_i(t):=\sum_k\gamma_i(t)_kA_k$ satisfies
$q(\gamma_i(t))=q(\mathbf 1)\det L_i(t)>0$, so $L_i(t)$ is nonsingular on
$[0,1]$; the eigenvalues of a continuous path of nonsingular real symmetric
matrices cannot change sign, so the inertia is constant.  At $t=1$,
$L_i(1)=A_1+A_2+A_3=I_m\succ0$, hence $A_i=L_i(0)\succ0$.  The two identities
are the determinantal representation evaluated at $\varepsilon_i$ and at
$(\varepsilon_i+\varepsilon_j)/2$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[\statusproved: hyperbolic Jensen--Stein pseudometric]\label{thm:lor-hyperbolic}
Let $p$ be hyperbolic as above.  Then $\sqrt{\J_p}$, with
$\J_p(x,y)=\log p\bigl(\frac{x+y}2\bigr)-\frac12\log p(x)-\frac12\log p(y)$, is a
pseudometric on $\Lambda_{++}$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Concavity of $F=p^{1/m}$ gives
$F(\frac{x+y}2)\ge\frac{F(x)+F(y)}2\ge\sqrt{F(x)F(y)}$, which after raising to
the $m$th power makes $\J_p\ge0$; symmetry and vanishing on the diagonal are
immediate.  For the triangle inequality fix $x_1,x_2,x_3\in\Lambda_{++}$ and take
$A_1,A_2,A_3\succ0$ from Lemma~\ref{lem:lor-pdrep}.  The factor $q(\mathbf 1)$
cancels, leaving
\[
\J_p(x_i,x_j)
=\log\frac{\det\bigl((A_i+A_j)/2\bigr)}{\sqrt{\det A_i\det A_j}}
=\delta_{\mathrm S}^2(A_i,A_j),
\]
the squared Stein divergence, whose square root is a metric on the positive
definite cone \citep{SraSdiv}.  Applying that triangle inequality to
$A_1,A_2,A_3$ gives the claim, and the triple was arbitrary.
\end{proof}

The conclusion is a pseudometric rather than a metric because degenerate $p$
collapse distances: for $p=\ell^m$ with $\ell$ linear, $\J_p(x,y)$ depends only on
$\ell(x)$ and $\ell(y)$.  The same restriction principle transfers a determinant
inequality to the cone.

\begin{theorem}[\statusproved: hyperbolic four-point inequality]\label{thm:lor-fourpoint}
With $F=p^{1/m}$, for all $a,b,c$ in the closed hyperbolicity cone,
\[
F(a+b)\,F(a+c)\;\ge\;F(a)\,F(a+b+c)+F(b)\,F(c).
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Write $\mathcal D(M)=\det(M)^{1/m}$.  We first prove the matrix form
$\mathcal D(A+B)\mathcal D(A+C)\ge\mathcal D(A)\mathcal D(A+B+C)+\mathcal D(B)\mathcal D(C)$
for $A,B,C\succeq0$.  For $A\succ0$, put $X=A^{-1/2}BA^{-1/2}$ and
$Y=A^{-1/2}CA^{-1/2}$; every term carries $\mathcal D(A)^2$, so it suffices to
show $\mathcal D(I+X)\mathcal D(I+Y)\ge\mathcal D(I+X+Y)+\mathcal D(X)\mathcal D(Y)$.
Order the eigenvalues of $X$ decreasingly and those of $Y$ increasingly.  Since a
product does not depend on the ordering,
$\mathcal D(I+X)\mathcal D(I+Y)=\bigl(\prod_i[(1+x_i+y_i)+x_iy_i]\bigr)^{1/m}$, and
Minkowski's determinant inequality
$\det(R+S)^{1/m}\ge\det R^{1/m}+\det S^{1/m}$ --- itself Jensen's inequality for
$t\mapsto\log(1+e^t)$ applied to the eigenvalues of $R^{-1/2}SR^{-1/2}$ ---
applied to the diagonal matrices $R=\diag(1+x_i+y_i)$ and $S=\diag(x_iy_i)$
gives
\[
\mathcal D(I+X)\mathcal D(I+Y)\ \ge\ \Bigl(\prod_i(1+x_i+y_i)\Bigr)^{1/m}
+\Bigl(\prod_ix_iy_i\Bigr)^{1/m}.
\]
Fiedler's upper bound \citep{Fiedler1971}, which pairs one decreasing spectrum
with the other increasing one, gives $\det(I+X+Y)\le\prod_i(1+x_i+y_i)$, while
the second term is $\mathcal D(X)\mathcal D(Y)$; a limit $\varepsilon\downarrow0$
in $A+\varepsilon I$ covers singular $A$.  For the cone statement, apply
Lemmas~\ref{lem:lor-ternary} and~\ref{lem:lor-pdrep} to $a,b,c\in\Lambda_{++}$ to
get $A,B,C\succ0$ with $p(ra+sb+tc)=q(\mathbf 1)\det(rA+sB+tC)$; then
$F(ra+sb+tc)=q(\mathbf 1)^{1/m}\mathcal D(rA+sB+tC)$ for $r,s,t\ge0$, and the
displayed inequality is the matrix one times $q(\mathbf 1)^{2/m}$.  Points of the
closed cone are handled by $u\mapsto u+\varepsilon e$, which lies in
$\Lambda_{++}$, and continuity.
\end{proof}

Nothing here collides with Theorem~\ref{thm:volume-distance} and
Corollary~\ref{thm:lorentzian}, and it is worth saying why not.  The witness
\eqref{eq:lorentzian-cubic} is strictly Lorentzian but \emph{not} hyperbolic:
$G(x,1)=\frac1{10}x^3+\frac32x^2+\frac{11}5x+1$ has discriminant
\[
18abcd-4b^3d+b^2c^2-4ac^3-27a^2d^2=-\frac{1499}{1250}<0
\qquad
\Bigl(a,b,c,d=\tfrac1{10},\tfrac32,\tfrac{11}5,1\Bigr),
\]
so it has one real and two complex conjugate roots.  A binary form is hyperbolic
with respect to some direction only if it splits into real linear factors --- each
factor $\ell_i$ contributes the root $-\ell_i(x)/\ell_i(e)$, which is real for
every real $x$ only when $\ell_i$ is real up to scale --- so $G$ is hyperbolic
with respect to no direction at all.  In two variables hyperbolicity is therefore
strictly stronger than strict Lorentzianity, and the witness lives in the gap.
That gap is the content of both results together: what makes the midpoint Jensen
gap a squared distance is the determinantal representation that hyperbolicity
buys through Helton--Vinnikov, and not log-concavity of the coefficients.
